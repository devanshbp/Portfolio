The system

The driver controls interface is everything the driver physically touches to operate the car - steering, the digital dash, the radio, and the seat bolstering around them. I owned this system for the 2026 competition car, working across CAD, structural validation, and hands-on manufacturing.

The full assembly breaks down into six major components: the steering assembly, steering wheel, control panel, headrest, radio mount, and seat bolsters.

Driver controls assembly - full CAD overview

Steering column redesign

My main focus this year was the steering column itself. The original design ran a straight shaft from the wheel to the rack - simple, but it fought the driver's seating position and introduced slop under load. I redesigned it around a double U-joint configuration, which let the column bend around the driver's legs and the pedal box instead of forcing the driver to accommodate the shaft.

Straight shaft (left) to double U-joint configuration (right)

The U-joint path improved packaging around the pedal box, reduced steering slop from the added compliance in a single long shaft, and gave the driver a more direct, predictable feel through the wheel.

Steering column U-joint system - full CAD detail

Control panel & dashboard

As part of the Driver Controls subsystem, I fully modeled the 2026 competition control panel/dashboard in Siemens NX, coordinating with the brake and chassis teams to integrate a brake bias adjustment knob and an emergency stop system directly into the wheel-mounted panel.

To validate the design before it went anywhere near a driver, I ran static structural analysis against the driver punch load case, the worst-case impact scenario for anything mounted in front of the driver, to confirm the panel and its mounts would hold up under that loading.

Control panel, emergency stop (red) and brake bias adjustment (orange)

From drawing to part

Once a design was validated, I manufactured the components myself from engineering drawings - CNC milling, manual lathe/mill work, and waterjet cutting, applying GD&T principles throughout to hold the tight dimensional tolerances the steering system needs. Balancing performance, manufacturability, and the team's build timeline meant iterating designs against test data and direct driver feedback rather than optimizing in a vacuum.

I presented the CAD models and supporting data analysis at both Preliminary Design Review (PDR) and Critical Design Review (CDR) to industry alumni and faculty evaluators.
